The memory landscape

Austria’s memorial landscape, counted. 4,393 documented places of remembrance — plaques, stones, monuments and sites of terror — extracted from the Digital Memorial Landscape and drawn at national scale.

01

A country of markers

A map of remembrance is not a neutral backdrop.

Each of the 4,393 points below is a record: a plaque mounted on a wall, a stone set into a pavement, a monument standing in a square. Each one is a decision — about who is named, what is said, and which piece of ground is asked to carry it.

The points are not spread evenly. Vienna alone holds 1,662 of the 4,393 records — more than a third — and the eastern provinces are dense; the western Alps are sparse. Part of that follows where people were persecuted, where camps, prisons and Gestapo offices stood. Part of it follows where later generations had the means and the will to put a marker on the ground.

232 records document sites of terror — camps, Gestapo and SS offices, prisons, courts, execution sites. They are drawn as squares, not dots. A memorial and a perpetrator site can stand on the same street, and a map must not make them look alike.

02

Three forms

A plaque attaches memory to a wall. A stone interrupts a pavement. A monument occupies a square.

The form decides how you meet the past: at eye level, underfoot, or at a distance. DERLA records all three, in its own vocabulary, alongside graves, streets and memorial stones.

  • 1,015

    Gedenktafel

    A panel fixed to a facade, a wall or a gate. Read standing.

  • 727

    Stolperstein

    A brass block set into the pavement, one per person. Read looking down.

  • 349

    Denkmal

    A free-standing structure in public space. Read approaching it.

The list is not a hierarchy. The dataset also holds 795 Steine der Erinnerung, 240 Gedenksteine and 201 graves — forms this study does not interpret. The categories are DERLA’s, not the author’s.

03

When memory happened

The map is not a record of the war. It is a record of what came after.

Of the 3,877 records that carry a date, 71 per cent are dated 2000 or later, and 1,481 fall in the 2010s alone. The curve below is therefore not a timeline of persecution. It is a timeline of the decision to mark it.

The first peak is immediate: 264 records date from 1945–49, and only two from 1940–44. The 1950s and 1960s are thin. The line rises through the 1980s and 1990s, then jumps. Several things meet in that jump — the end of the eyewitness generation, a changed public argument about who belongs in the story, and the arrival of projects that set stones street by street.

This chart cannot say which of those caused what. It can say that most of what is marked in Austria was marked in the last twenty-five years.

04

What the map cannot show

4,393 dots are not 4,393 stories.

The dataset records markers, not every place where something happened. 174 of the sites are not publicly accessible. 377 records carry no object identifier, so they cannot be opened from the map at all. And a national map at one scale says nothing about how a place reads on the street — whether a plaque is legible, whether a stone has been cleaned, whether anyone stops.

The gaps are part of the landscape, not an error in it. A marker needs an address, a budget and a decision, and every place that never received one is absent from this dataset as well. DERLA’s curated paths carry the routes this page deliberately does not reproduce. Start there, not here:

  • Wege der Erinnerung — curated routes
  • Karte der Erinnerung — the full map
  • DERLA — the project

This page is an overview, not an authority. Where it and DERLA differ, DERLA is right.
