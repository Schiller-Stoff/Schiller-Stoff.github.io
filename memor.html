The last addresses

MEMOR, the City of Graz’s digital memorial book, records 812 people persecuted under National Socialism. 808 of them are placed at the address where they last lived — 604 distinct addresses inside the present-day city boundary. This page draws that register at the scale of a city, and counts what happened after it.

01

The last addresses

808 of the 812 people in the register have a last voluntary address.

MEMOR places people where they lived before persecution forced them from their homes, not where they were taken. Those 808 records resolve to 604 distinct addresses, all inside the boundary of the present-day city of Graz. Four people in the register have no last voluntary address at all: three whose entry carries no address, and one with no recorded event in the public extract. They are still in the book.

The distribution is not even. Of the 604 addresses, 351 are in three central districts — Gries, Lend and Jakomini — and the districts beyond them hold far fewer. That pattern follows where people lived in the 1930s, and it is the first thing the map below makes visible.

Read as a table, the same register has a clear centre of gravity. These are the 604 addresses by today’s municipal district. The district boundaries are the City of Graz’s; the assignment is a point-in-polygon check, not a claim by MEMOR.

Beyond the six central districts the register thins quickly: the ten that follow hold 77 addresses between them, 28 of them in Eggenberg alone, and the nine smallest hold 49. Ries, the tenth district, holds none.

An address in this register is a present-day geocode of a historical address. The building may be gone, the street renumbered, the ground rebuilt. A point marks where a record is located, not a doorstep to stand on.

02

The forced addresses

496 people are recorded at a forced address. The register holds 161 distinct forced addresses, and most of them are not in Graz.

242 of the records are the Landes-Heil- und Pflegeanstalt „Am Feldhof“ in Straßgang, a psychiatric institution. 173 are at 109 addresses in Vienna’s second district; 57 more are elsewhere in Vienna. Five are at the Salzburg-Maxglan forced-labour camp, two in the occupied Netherlands and one at the Steinhof institution in Vienna. Sixteen records fall at nine other Graz addresses.

  1. Feldhof institution, Graz242
  2. Vienna, second district173
  3. Vienna, elsewhere57
  4. Other Graz addresses16
  5. Salzburg-Maxglan5
  6. Occupied Netherlands2
  7. Steinhof institution, Vienna1

This chapter has no map, and that is deliberate. A city outline with nine points on it — the nine non-Feldhof addresses in Graz, held by sixteen people — would make forced residence look like a minor local phenomenon. It was not. The addresses are where the register follows people after their last voluntary home: to institutions, camps and the flats of relatives in other cities. Drawing them on Graz would misstate both the geography and the scale.

The wording matters here. The register records someone at a forced address; it does not document a transfer, a route or a sequence. This page therefore says “registered at”, never “sent to”. The one relation the data supports is the subject of chapter 04.

No residential address from this chapter is reproduced. Institutions and districts are named because they are historical sites, not homes.

03

Where they died

668 people have a recorded place of death, at 74 sites. 143 do not.

The register’s 668 death records carry 83 different place names. Grouping variant spellings — „Tötungsanstalt Hartheim“, „Hartheim“ — gives 74 sites. That grouping is editorial and happens in the offline generator, not in MEMOR; where this page and MEMOR differ, MEMOR is right.

The top ten hold 513 of the 668 deaths. Behind them are 64 more sites, from Mauthausen and Sobibór to the Graz-Wetzelsdorf SS barracks and the execution site at Ligist.

346 of the 668 deaths — 52 per cent — were outside the borders of today’s Austria, in fourteen countries. 322 were inside. The largest groups outside Austria are in Poland, Germany, Czechia and Belarus.

Distance makes the same point less abstractly. For the 666 people who have both a last voluntary address and a recorded death place, the straight-line distance between the two has a mean of 372 km. 170 people died more than 500 km from home; 46 more than 1,000 km.

04

The Hartheim year

1941 holds 204 of the 583 deaths with a single year. 172 of them are at Hartheim.

590 of the 668 death records carry a date. 583 give one year; seven give a range such as 1941/42 and are left out of the chart; 78 carry no date at all. The shape below is therefore the shape of the dated record, not of every death in the register.

Hartheim’s dated records run 1940: 38, 1941: 172, 1942: 4. That is 214 dated deaths out of 247, so the year is a floor, not a total. Of the 204 deaths dated 1941, 172 — 84 per cent — are at Hartheim. The peak belongs to one site, not to the register as a whole.

242 of the 247 people whose deaths are recorded at Hartheim were last registered at Feldhof. The register supports that sequence of two entries and nothing more: no date of transfer, no transport, no route. This page does not draw a line between the two places, because the data does not.

The killing facility at Hartheim was in Upper Austria, not in Graz. The Graz register reaches it because the people recorded at Feldhof were registered there before their deaths were recorded at Hartheim.

05

What the map cannot show

A register records what was documented, not everything that happened.

For 143 people the register records no place of death. That is the whole of the statement: the record ends at the address or the forced address, and the page will not turn a gap into a fate. Nothing here implies survival, and nothing implies a death that was not recorded.

78 death records carry no date, and seven give a range of years. One of the 812 people in the register has no event in the public extract that feeds this page, so 811 appear in the counts. The register is the authority for all 812.

The addresses are present-day geocodes of historical addresses. The building may be gone; the street may have been renumbered or renamed. A dot marks where a record is located, not the house where someone lived. The map also cannot show what the categories in the register mean: 410 people are recorded as Jewish victims, 244 under the euthanasia category, 80 as political resistance, and 20 people fall into more than one category. Those are MEMOR’s categories, overlapping on purpose. This page does not regroup them.

No names, portraits or house numbers are reproduced in this study. Individuals belong in MEMOR, which can show a biography and be corrected. Start there, not here:
